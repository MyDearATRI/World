A distance distinguishes how far points are apart. Many arguments use only which sets contain a neighborhood of a point. A topology records exactly that information, allowing continuity to survive after a distance has been forgotten or was never available.

## DEF — Topology and local information

A topology $\mathcal T$ on a set $X$ contains $\varnothing,X$, is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Its members are open; complements of open sets are closed. A neighborhood of $x$ is any set containing an open set that contains $x$; it need not itself be open. For $A\subset X$, the relative topology is $\{A\cap U:U\in\mathcal T\}$. Thus a set may be relatively closed in $A$ without being closed in $X$.

The interior $A^\circ$ is the union of open sets contained in $A$. The closure $\overline A$ is the intersection of closed sets containing $A$, and the boundary is $\partial A=\overline A\setminus A^\circ$. Equivalently, $x\in\overline A$ exactly when every neighborhood of $x$ meets $A$. A point is an accumulation point of $A$ if every neighborhood meets $A\setminus\{x\}$; if $A'$ denotes these points, then $\overline A=A\cup A'$. Density in $B$ means $B\subset\overline A$, not necessarily $A\subset B$ under Simon's terminology.

For example, $(0,1)$ is open in $\mathbb R$, while $[0,1)$ is relatively open in $[0,2]$. In the indiscrete topology $\{\varnothing,X\}$, every nonempty set has closure $X$. In the discrete topology every subset is both open and closed. These extremes show how little separation is built into the axioms.

## PROP — Closure as an alternative specification

Closure satisfies $\overline\varnothing=\varnothing$, $A\subset\overline A$, $\overline{\overline A}=\overline A$, and $\overline{A\cup B}=\overline A\cup\overline B$. Conversely, an operator obeying these four Kuratowski axioms determines a unique topology by declaring its fixed sets closed. The construction uses monotonicity, which follows from the finite-union axiom; intersections of fixed sets are fixed because the closure of their intersection is contained in every member. The full equivalence is the task of §2.1, Problem 1; this paragraph identifies the proof mechanism rather than supplying that exercise's complete solution.

A base for a topology $\mathcal T$ is a family $\mathcal B\subset\mathcal T$ such that every open set is a union of members of $\mathcal B$. More generally, a family of subsets covering $X$ and satisfying the following refinement condition generates a topology by arbitrary unions: whenever $x\in B_1\cap B_2$, some $B_3\in\mathcal B$ satisfies $x\in B_3\subset B_1\cap B_2$. To be a base for a previously specified topology $\mathcal T$, those unions must equal $\mathcal T$. A subbase generates a base by finite intersections, including $X$ as the empty intersection. Arbitrary intersections of open sets need not be open: $\bigcap_n(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\}$ in $\mathbb R$.

## THM — Continuity is an inverse-image property

A map $f:X\to Y$ is continuous when $f^{-1}(U)$ is open for every open $U\subset Y$. It suffices to check a base or a subbase of $Y$, since inverse images preserve unions and intersections. Equivalently, inverse images of closed sets are closed. A homeomorphism is a bijection for which both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are continuous; mere continuous bijectivity is insufficient, as the identity from discrete $\mathbb R$ to usual $\mathbb R$ shows.

Continuity implies preservation of convergent sequences: for a neighborhood $U$ of $f(x)$, $f^{-1}(U)$ is a neighborhood of $x$, so $x_n\to x$ makes $f(x_n)$ eventually lie in $U$. The converse needs additional hypotheses. The metric case is proved in [[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第一章/知识/度量拓扑与连续性的三种刻画|the three metric continuity criteria]]; [[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第二章/知识/Bases and Countability Conditions|first countability]] explains what survives, and [[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第二章/知识/Nets and General Convergence|nets]] recover an unrestricted criterion.

Given maps $f_i:X\to Y_i$, the weakest topology making them all continuous is generated by the sets $f_i^{-1}(U_i)$. This initial or weak topology is a topology on the domain $X$. Its direction matters: the [[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第二章/知识/Quotient Topologies|quotient construction]] instead equips a codomain with the strongest topology compatible with a given map.

## THM — Uniform limits and complete function spaces

Let $X$ be any topological space and $(Y,d)$ a metric space. A sequence of continuous maps $f_n:X\to Y$ converging uniformly to $f$ has a continuous limit. Fix $x$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $n$ so $d(f_n(z),f(z))<\varepsilon/3$ for every $z$. Continuity of this one $f_n$ provides a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ on which $d(f_n(z),f_n(x))<\varepsilon/3$. The triangle inequality then gives $d(f(z),f(x))<\varepsilon$ on $U$. The same index must work for every $z$; pointwise convergence cannot supply that step.

If $Y$ is complete, the bounded continuous maps $C_b(X,Y)$ form a complete metric space under $d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_xd(f(x),g(x))$, with $d_\infty=0$ when $X$ is empty. For a Cauchy sequence $(f_n)$, each $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy, so define $f(x)=\lim_n f_n(x)$. Given $\varepsilon$, choose $N$ with $d_\infty(f_n,f_m)\le\varepsilon$ for $m,n\ge N$. Passing $m\to\infty$ pointwise yields $d_\infty(f_n,f)\le\varepsilon$. Thus convergence is uniform, the previous argument gives continuity, and a uniform bound relative to one $f_N$ gives boundedness. Completeness of $Y$ is used to create the pointwise limit; uniform Cauchy control keeps the result within the function space.

For contrast, $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to a function discontinuous at $1$. This example will matter again when separating uniform approximation from the convergence of a specified Fourier expansion.
