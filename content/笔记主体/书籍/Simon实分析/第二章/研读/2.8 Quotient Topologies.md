Identifying points creates a quotient set, but it does not yet decide what continuity means on that set. The quotient topology makes a subset open exactly when its full inverse image in the original space is open. Saturation by complete equivalence classes is therefore part of every allowed observation.

## Factorization with topology

For the class map $q:X\to X/{\sim}$, a map $g:X/{\sim}\to Y$ is continuous exactly when $g\circ q$ is continuous. A continuous map constant on classes thus descends uniquely and continuously. This extends the [[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第一章/知识/等价关系与商构造|set-theoretic quotient construction]] by adding a continuity statement rather than replacing its well-definedness check.

Suppose $f:X\to Y$ is continuous and onto and the classes are its fibers. The induced map from the quotient to $Y$ is a continuous bijection. If $f$ is also open, it is a homeomorphism. The [[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第二章/知识/Quotient Topologies|quotient topology page]] gives the proof and a continuous-bijection counterexample that shows why the extra condition matters.

## What is erased by identification

The relation of rational difference on $[0,1]$ has dense classes. A nonempty closed saturated set therefore fills the entire original space, so the quotient topology is indiscrete. The original compact Hausdorff space has produced a compact quotient that is no longer Hausdorff. Compactness survives by continuous images; separation does not.

The relation $R=\{(x,y):x\sim y\}$ must be closed if the quotient is Hausdorff. Closedness of $R$ together with openness of $q$ is sufficient: a product neighborhood avoiding $R$ descends to disjoint quotient neighborhoods. The same condition can be used for group actions, whose orbit quotient maps are open when the action is by homeomorphisms. An irrational rotation of the circle again has dense orbits and an indiscrete quotient.

## Keep the construction distinct from completion

The quotient map identifies existing objects. In metric completion, the objects are first enlarged to Cauchy sequences, and only then identified. This distinction explains why a quotient of the original rational points cannot supply missing irrational limits. It also prevents treating every quotient topology as a metric zero-distance quotient.
