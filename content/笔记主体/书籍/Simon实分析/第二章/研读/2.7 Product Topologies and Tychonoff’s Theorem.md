An infinite product requires a decision about which coordinate conditions count as a neighborhood. The product topology allows finitely many restrictions at a time. This choice supports coordinatewise convergence and is indispensable to the compactness theorem.

## Finite-coordinate tests

The topology is generated by inverse images under coordinate projections. A net converges exactly when all its coordinates converge, because finitely many eventual thresholds have a common upper bound. A map into a product is continuous exactly when all coordinate maps are continuous. A cluster point, however, requires simultaneous finite-coordinate tests: separate cluster behavior in each coordinate need not happen at the same indices.

[[笔记主体/书籍/Simon实分析/第二章/知识/Products and Compactness|Products and compactness]] gives these statements and the compatible metric for a countable product of metric spaces. In a countable product of compact metric spaces, the diagonal subsequence method from Chapter 1 yields compactness directly. The proof does not extend simply by writing an uncountable list of extraction stages.

## Maximal compatible partial limits

For the general compact Hausdorff product, start with a net and consider partial cluster points on subsets of coordinates. Order them by extension. A chain has a compatible coordinatewise union, but this union is useful only after verifying it remains a cluster point. Every basic neighborhood inspects finitely many coordinates, so one chain member contains all the coordinates of that test. This verifies the chain condition for Zorn.

A maximal partial cluster point cannot omit a coordinate: first take a subnet converging in its existing coordinates, then use compactness of the omitted factor to take a further subnet converging in that coordinate. The extension contradicts maximality. The full point is a cluster point of the original net, and net compactness finishes the proof. The knowledge page expands the finite-test step omitted in the source's compressed chain argument.

## What changes under a stronger topology

The box topology on $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}$ is discrete and not compact, despite each factor being finite and compact. An uncountable product $[0,1]^A$ in the product topology is compact but not metrizable: any countable list of basic neighborhoods leaves coordinates untested, so singletons are not $G_\delta$ sets. These examples distinguish compactness, metrizability, and the chosen product topology.
